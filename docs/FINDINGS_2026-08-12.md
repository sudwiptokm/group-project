# Why the peak results do not hold, and what replaces them

Written 2026-08-12. Supersedes the peak half of `RESULTS_WRITEUP.md`. The
off-peak results in that document still stand; nothing here contradicts them.

All measurements below are reproducible from the scripts named in each section.

---

## Summary

The headline Stage-1 claim — *DQN and A2C cut mean waiting time ~24% at peak* —
does not survive scrutiny. Six defects were found, five of them independent of
each other and any one sufficient to invalidate the number. Correcting them
reverses the result: **no learned policy beats a competently timed fixed plan at
this intersection, and the gap is a factor of 2-3.**

The reason turns out to be structural rather than a training failure, and it is
the most useful thing this project has produced.

---

## 1. The headline metric was a gridlock clock

`system_mean_waiting_time` (sumo-rl's default, and the project's ranking key)
averages `traci.vehicle.getWaitingTime()` over vehicles **still in the network**,
then time-averages that over the episode. Two consequences:

* It is survivorship-biased. Vehicles that clear the junction stop contributing;
  vehicles stuck in a jam keep contributing forever.
* Under gridlock it degenerates into a clock. Once the network locks, the
  counter climbs at exactly 1 s per simulated second.

Peak deadlocked at t = 780 s (629 vehicles, mean speed ~1e-5 m/s). The reported
1319 s is the area under that ramp, not a delay.

It also inverted the ranking. A2C deadlocked on 5 of 5 seeds and scored *best*;
QR-DQN kept traffic moving (~0.7 m/s, half the queue) and scored *worst*.

## 2. The baseline ran on different traffic than the agents

`env_common.make_env` passes `sumo_seed=seed`, so the demand realisation is
seed-dependent. Stage 1 evaluated the fixed-time baseline on seed 0 only, and
the RL agents on seeds 42-46.

Running the baseline on the agents' own seeds (`baseline.py --seed 42..46`):

| seed | 0 | 42 | 43 | 44 | 45 | 46 |
|------|---|----|----|----|----|----|
| fixed-time mean wait (s) | 1319 | 1081 | 242 | 920 | 986 | 1203 |

A 5.4x spread, and seed 0 was the worst draw of the six. Comparing each agent
against the baseline on its *own* seed:

| algo  | reported | paired, all 5 seeds | excl. seed 43 | seeds beaten |
|-------|---------:|--------------------:|--------------:|-------------:|
| dqn   |   -23.9% |              +56.9% |         -6.2% |          1/5 |
| a2c   |   -23.9% |              +67.6% |         -6.4% |          3/5 |
| ppo   |    +2.8% |             +118.4% |        +30.5% |          0/5 |
| qrdqn |    +6.2% |             +123.3% |        +35.2% |          0/5 |

Reproduce with `analysis/paired.py`.

## 3. Every evaluated model predates the safety fix

Models are dated 26-30 Jul; commit `1a678e51` (6 Aug) fixed safety sampling.
Before it: exposure was unreachable by construction (`delta_time`=5 >
`yellow_time`=3, and sumo-rl asserts that ordering), braking was sampled once per
5 s on the settled post-yellow second and so biased low, and `SAFETY_SCALE` was
0.024 against today's 2.1298. The commit measured brake 0.206 -> 5.07 and
exposure 0.0 -> 11.57.

The 6 Aug pass re-evaluated the old checkpoints and re-logged correct metrics. It
did not retrain. Every reported number therefore comes from a policy optimised
against a reward the project no longer uses.

## 4. "std over 5 seeds" was not seed variance

`run_experiment.sh:145` evaluates only `models/<algo>_<tag>_seed$REF_SEED.zip`,
where `REF_SEED` is the first training seed. Four of the five models trained per
cell were never evaluated at all.

So the reported +/-402 is one policy's spread across five demand realisations,
not spread across training seeds, contrary to how `RESULTS_WRITEUP.md` describes
it. Fixed by `train.py --train-seed`, which suffixes the eval CSV `_t<n>`.

## 5. The gridlock was a library default, not the demand

sumo-rl sets `time_to_teleport = -1` (teleporting off); SUMO's own default is
300 s. With teleporting disabled, junction deadlock is an **absorbing state** —
the 2-phase program has permissive left turns (`GGGgrrrr`), so once the junction
locks nothing ever clears it.

A demand sweep from 0.9x to 1.4x base is bimodal and non-monotonic — either free
flow (~0.5 s) or deadlock (~1200 s), with nothing in between, and 1.4x/seed 42
milder than 1.1x/seed 42. That is a stochastic tipping process, not a demand
level.

At `--time-to-teleport 300`, peak 1.5x becomes stable and measurable: **42.4 +/-
10.4 s** over seeds 42-46, ~9 teleports per episode, no deadlock on any seed.
**Peak demand needs no change.** Exposed as `make_env(teleport=)` /
`TIME_TO_TELEPORT`, defaulting to -1 so existing callers are unaffected.

Reproduce with `analysis/probe_demand.py`.

## 6. The "fixed-time" baseline was not a fixed-time plan

`baseline.py:23` advances the action every decision step:

```python
action = (action + 1) % n_actions   # round-robin green phases = fixed-time
```

With `delta_time`=5 and `min_green`=10 that is a **10 s-green cycler**, not the
42 s static program in `intersection.net.xml`, and not what fixed-time control
means in traffic engineering. Every "vs fixed-time" comparison in the project is
against that controller.

## What actually limits performance

Sweeping static green durations at peak (`analysis/static_timing.py`, 1200 s
episodes, teleport 300, mean waiting time in seconds):

| green (s) | seed 42 | seed 43 | seed 44 | mean | notes |
|-----------|--------:|--------:|--------:|-----:|-------|
| 10        |   48.25 |    5.67 |   26.15 | 26.7 | = the Stage-1 "fixed-time" baseline |
| 15        |   41.96 |   30.71 |   22.69 | 31.8 | |
| 20        |   22.01 |   27.41 |   20.78 | 23.4 | |
| 30        |   51.02 |    5.21 |   17.56 | 24.6 | |
| 45        |    9.24 |   27.23 |    8.70 | 15.1 | |
| **60**    |   12.06 |   11.82 |   10.71 | **11.5** | best; spread only +/-0.68 |

For comparison, the learned policies score 20-33 s on the same environment.

**A static 60 s plan beats every learned policy by a factor of 2-3, and is far
more consistent across seeds.**

### `max_green` is not a constraint

sumo-rl's `TrafficSignal` stores `max_green` and never reads it again —
`traffic_signal.py:77` is its only occurrence in the class. The `max_green=60`
configured in `env_common.make_env` therefore binds nothing, for a static plan or
for a learned policy. Two consequences:

* the static sweep has to run past 60 s to claim an optimum, and does;
* a documented parameter that silently does nothing is worth reporting on its
  own, alongside the `time_to_teleport` default in section 5. Both are defects
  in how sumo-rl is commonly used rather than in this codebase.

### Mechanism: lost time to amber

`yellow_time` is 3 s. Each phase switch spends 3 s serving nobody.

| green | lost time |
|-------|-----------|
| 10 s  | 3/13 = **23%** |
| 20 s  | 3/23 = 13% |
| 60 s  | 3/63 = **4.8%** |

The agent decides every 5 s with a 10 s minimum green, so it operates precisely
in the region where switching is cheap to attempt and very expensive to pay for.
`diff_waiting_time` rewards the immediate queue drop on the served approach and
only registers the lost-time cost several decisions later, by which point credit
assignment is hopeless at this sample budget.

This single mechanism explains the Stage-1 result, the pilot, and the 20k-step
null together. It is not a training-budget artefact.

### Ruled out: teleport-corrupted reward

Hypothesis: a teleport erases a jammed vehicle's accumulated waiting time,
producing a large unearned positive reward. Rejected — reward on teleport steps
is mostly *more* negative (-19.1, -18.5, -13.3) with occasional positives (+15.4,
+2.0). Teleports occur when the jam is already bad. The reward function is not
corrupted; the policy space is simply unfavourable.

## Training attempted, and why it stopped

`run_lambda_sweep.sh` (new, resumable, signal-hardened) was written to run the
lambda ablation the project never had — Stage 1 only ever ran lambda=0.5.

Two attempts, both abandoned:

1. **20k steps with `params/*.json`.** Those files were tuned for a 100k-step
   budget: lr ~2.3e-5, dqn `learning_starts`=5000, `target_update_interval`=5000.
   Against 20k steps that is 25% of the budget on random actions and 3 target
   updates. Nothing learned; episode mean wait was flat or rising on all seeds.
2. **20k steps with the repo defaults** (`--defaults`; dqn lr 1e-4 / starts 1000
   / target 1000, ppo lr 3e-4 / n_steps 256). An 8k single-seed pilot appeared to
   show PPO learning (25.7 -> 20.3 across quartiles). It did not replicate: at
   20k over three seeds PPO was flat to worse (first-5 -> last-5: 21.2 -> 32.5,
   23.9 -> 32.8, 11.9 -> 23.8), and tensorboard `ep_rew_mean` was flat at
   -640 -> -654. The pilot's apparent signal was single-seed noise — the same
   error this document criticises in section 2.

Given the static-timing result, more training is not the missing ingredient.

## Measured throughput, for anyone re-running this

Apple M1 Pro, 8 cores / 8 GB, single process: **dqn 6.43 steps/s, ppo 7.32
steps/s**. A 20k-step run is ~50-60 min. At JOBS=3 (the most an 8 GB machine
takes without swapping) an 18-run grid is ~7 h.

## What should be reported

Defensible without further compute:

* The off-peak result. Fixed-time 0.40 +/- 0.00 s across 6 seeds; no RL agent
  beats it; A2C fails catastrophically (+8910%). At 0.40 s there is no headroom,
  so this conclusion is robust to the reward defect in section 3.
* Sections 1, 2, 4, 5, 6 as methodology findings. Each is measured, each
  generalises past this project, and three of them are defects in how sumo-rl is
  commonly used rather than in this codebase.
* The static-timing result and the lost-time mechanism, as the explanation for
  why RL does not help at a 2-phase single intersection.

Not defensible: any peak performance claim from Stage 1, and the lambda ablation,
which has never been run.

## If the work continues

1. ~~Switch the headline metric to completed-trip delay and throughput from
   `tripinfo`.~~ **Done.** `make_env(tripinfo=True)` writes one row per completed
   trip beside each eval CSV; `analysis/tripinfo.py` reduces it; `compare.py`
   ranks on `trip_time_loss_mean` and reports throughput and completion rate.
   On the same peak runs the two metrics disagree by 4x — 14.97 s in-network
   against 66.43 s of waiting per completed trip — which is section 1's bias
   measured directly rather than argued.
2. ~~Re-baseline against the best static plan (60 s), not the 10 s cycler.~~
   **Done.** `baseline.py --green` (default = the best static plan), with the
   green in the CSV name so two plans cannot be averaged into one "fixed-time"
   row; `compare.py` warns if a logs dir mixes them. Peak, seeds 42-46, 3600 s
   episodes, teleport 300: **91.8 +/- 19.9 s delay per completed trip**, 4076
   trips, 94.3% of demand completed.
3. Give the agent a policy space worth searching. Options, cheapest first: raise
   `min_green` so short switches are unreachable; add a protected left-turn phase
   (4 phases instead of 2); move to the corridor environment, where coordination
   between junctions is something a static plan cannot do well.
4. Only then re-tune and run the lambda ablation.

Item 3 matters most. At a 2-phase isolated junction there is little for a learned
controller to win, and that is worth stating as a result rather than treating as
a bug.
